INTERVIEWER: Thank you for sitting down with me, Ms. Reyes. Could we start with your name and where you grew up?

SPEAKER: My name's Alma Reyes, and I grew up in South Chicago, right in the shadow of the mill. Illinois has been home my whole life. I was born October 12th, 1957, in a house on Buffalo Avenue that my father put up himself, one room at a time.

INTERVIEWER: What brought the family there?

SPEAKER: Work, same as everybody. My father, Salvador, came up from Guanajuato in 1948 and got on at the steel mill. He was a millwright, and he was proud of it. My mother, Esperanza, took in sewing at the kitchen table — everybody on the block called her Pera. She could have four dresses going at once and never lose a pin.

INTERVIEWER: Did you work at the mill yourself?

SPEAKER: I did. I started at nineteen, in the office, not out on the floor. My brother Rafael went out on the floor, and he stayed the longest of any of us. When the mill shut in 1992 we all scattered — some to the North Side, some clean out of Chicago. It took the neighborhood with it.

INTERVIEWER: How did people manage after that?

SPEAKER: However they could. I went back to school at thirty-four and became a nurse. I worked at Saint Anthony Hospital until I retired. My daughter Marisol is a teacher now, over in Pilsen — she's the first one of us with a master's degree, and I would like that written down.

INTERVIEWER: You said the neighborhood changed.

SPEAKER: It emptied out. Reagan was president when the layoffs started, and people still argue about that at the table. Then after 9/11 there was a lot of talk about bringing industry back, and nothing ever came of it. Talk is cheap on the South Side.

INTERVIEWER: Are you still in touch with people from those years?

SPEAKER: Oh, plenty of them. I'm not hard to find. The house phone is 773-555-0182, same number since 1981. Email's alma.reyes@example.org if you'd rather do it that way. My daughter set up an account for the parish, @stanthonysouth, and she puts the pictures up on there.

INTERVIEWER: And your mother, is she still with us?

SPEAKER: She passed a few years ago. She was ninety-one, and sharp the whole way. My father went before her, in his seventies. He never did retire, not really.

INTERVIEWER: Is there anything you want people to understand about that time?

SPEAKER: That we built something out here, and it wasn't handed to us. I still have my union card — the number is 4-772-9930 — and I am not ashamed of a bit of it. We were Mexican and we were from here, both at the same time. People act like you have to pick one.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you, Ms. Reyes. This has been a real gift.

SPEAKER: Thank you for coming out and asking.
