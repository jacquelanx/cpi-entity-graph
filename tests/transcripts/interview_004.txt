INTERVIEWER: You've talked before about your recovery. Can you walk me through it?

SPEAKER: Yeah. It started getting bad in my early twenties. I was twenty-two when I first tried to get clean, and it didn't take. My mom, Diane, never gave up on me, even when I gave her every reason to. She's in her late fifties now and still worries like I'm a teenager.

INTERVIEWER: Who else was in your corner?

SPEAKER: My sister Kayla, mostly. She'd drive two hours to sit with me. Kayla's son Elijah was just a baby then, and holding him gave me something to fight for. I have a brother too, we call him Chip, but he was going through his own stuff, so he couldn't be there the same way. No hard feelings. Everybody's fighting something.

INTERVIEWER: Was there a turning point?

SPEAKER: Three years ago. I finally checked into a program in Baton Rouge. My counselor Dana ran the group sessions, and for the first time I actually talked. My doctor, Dr. Nguyen, got my meds right, which mattered more than people understand. And my sponsor, Ray, he calls me every morning still. Ray's saved my life more times than he'll admit.

INTERVIEWER: Where were you living then?

SPEAKER: Back in New Orleans, in a halfway house. There was a woman who ran it, the one who took me in during that stretch, Miss Bea. She wasn't family, but she treated us like we were hers. She'd cook Sunday dinner for anybody who showed up. I don't know where I'd be without her.

INTERVIEWER: Did anything outside of the program help?

SPEAKER: Sounds silly, but I saw Oprah talk about addiction on TV once, really honestly, and it cracked something open in me. Made me feel less like a monster. During COVID it got hard again, being isolated, but by then I had the tools. Dana had drilled them into me. Ray kept calling.

INTERVIEWER: How's the family now?

SPEAKER: Better than I deserve. Kayla and I are close. Elijah's ten now and calls me his favorite. Diane cries every time I hit another year clean. Chip and I are rebuilding, slowly. It's real. I've been chasing this feeling of being trusted again, and it's finally coming back.

INTERVIEWER: What do you tell people who are where you were?

SPEAKER: That you can't do it alone, and you're not supposed to. Find your Diane, your Miss Bea, your Ray. I've been in and out of that darkness since I was a kid, honestly, and the only thing that ever worked was letting people in. Dr. Nguyen told me once that healing isn't a straight line. I hold onto that on the bad days.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you for being so open.

SPEAKER: It's the least I can do. Somebody was open with me once, and it saved me.
