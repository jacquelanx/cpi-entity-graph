INTERVIEWER: Thanks for sitting down with me. Maybe start with where you grew up?

SPEAKER: Sure. I was born in New Orleans, over in the Ninth Ward, in March 1990. My mom raised me mostly on her own. Her name was Gloria, but honestly everybody just called her Glo, even the teachers. She worked nights, so my aunt Maria watched us a lot of the time. Maria was my mom's older sister, and she basically lived at our place. Her husband, Ronnie, worked down at Charity Hospital as an orderly for years, and he'd bring home these stories.

INTERVIEWER: What was the neighborhood like?

SPEAKER: Close-knit. There was a woman down the block, Miss Rosa, who watched half the kids on the street. I still don't know her last name, we all just called her Miss Rosa. And there was a guy named Tank, big dude, who ran the corner store. His real name was Terrence but nobody used it. Tank looked out for us.

INTERVIEWER: Did your family stay in the city?

SPEAKER: Most of us, until the storm. Right after Hurricane Katrina we scattered. My cousin Denise ended up in Houston and never came back. Maria and Ronnie went to Baton Rouge for about a year, then came home. I was about nineteen then, so it hit during a weird time for me. A few years back I finally moved out to the suburbs myself, but the Ninth Ward is home.

INTERVIEWER: You mentioned teachers. Anyone stand out?

SPEAKER: My teacher Mr. Alvarez, he taught history. He's the one who got me reading. And later my counselor, Ms. Patterson, pushed me to apply to college when nobody else did. I owe her a lot. Funny enough, my best friend from back then, we've known each other since we were little, her name is Beyonce, no relation to the famous one, everyone teased her about it. She's a nurse now.

INTERVIEWER: Do you follow politics much?

SPEAKER: Some. I remember the year Obama got elected, the whole block was out celebrating. I even shook hands with Mayor Landrieu at a community event once, which felt like a big deal at the time. But I'm not really into all that.

INTERVIEWER: And now? Family of your own?

SPEAKER: Yeah. My wife, Carla, and I have two kids. My daughter Sofia just turned eight, and the little one, we call him Junior, he's three. Carla's mom, Rosa, lives with us now, different Rosa from Miss Rosa, I know it's confusing. I'm in my early thirties now, trying to give them what my mom gave me. My brother Marcus lives close too, so the kids see their uncle a lot. We do Sunday dinners like Gloria used to.

INTERVIEWER: That's beautiful. Thank you.

SPEAKER: Thank you for listening. It's good to talk about them.

INTERVIEWER: Tell me about your family coming to the States.

SPEAKER: My father, Hector, came first, in March 3, 2003. He worked two jobs before he could bring the rest of us over. My mother Lucia and my brother Roberto followed later. We called Roberto "Beto" growing up, still do actually. Beto was always the responsible one. When we finally settled in Los Angeles, it felt like the whole world had opened up.

INTERVIEWER: What did your parents do for work?

SPEAKER: My dad admired Obama, used to say America gave him a fair shot. He cleaned offices at night and did landscaping during the day. My mom took care of houses. I helped when I could. By the time I was twenty-five, I'd saved enough to open a little restaurant with Beto. My boss before that, Mr. Kim, taught me everything about running a kitchen. He was tough but fair, and I still call him for advice.

INTERVIEWER: How did the restaurant go?

SPEAKER: Slow at first. We had regulars, though. There was a customer named Grace who came in every single day for years. She'd sit at the counter and tell us about her grandkids. When the pandemic hit, we almost lost everything. That stretch was brutal. We stayed open doing takeout, and honestly Grace and a few others kept us alive by ordering constantly.

INTERVIEWER: Is the family close now?

SPEAKER: Very. Roberto's wife, Elena, does the books for the restaurant. She's sharp, way better with numbers than either of us. My niece Camila, their daughter, works weekends at the register. She's twelve, so mostly she just charms the customers. My mom, she's in her sixties now, still comes in to supervise, whether we want her to or not.

INTERVIEWER: Any traditions you've kept?

SPEAKER: Sunday meals, always. Lucia cooks the old recipes. There's a photo of Beyonce on the wall, my niece put it up, she's a huge fan. And we keep a picture of my father near the register. He passed a few years ago, but it still feels like he's watching the place.

INTERVIEWER: Where did you grow up before LA?

SPEAKER: A bit in Chicago, honestly back when I was a teenager it's a blur. We moved so much. But Los Angeles is where we planted roots. My kids were born here. I want them to know where Hector started, cleaning those offices, so they never take it for granted.

INTERVIEWER: What would your dad think of the restaurant now?

SPEAKER: He'd be proud, I think. He'd probably tell me to work harder anyway. That was Hector. Lucia says I sound just like him when I lecture the staff. Maybe she's right. Family is the whole thing for us. Everything we built, we built together, and every plate that goes out has a little of that history in it. That's what I'd want people to understand about us.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you so much.

SPEAKER: Of course. Anytime.

INTERVIEWER: Let's talk about who raised you.

SPEAKER: My grandfather William did, mostly. Everyone called him Will, or Will Senior when my dad was around. He was a quiet man, a machinist. My grandmother passed when I was young, her name was Ruth, and I barely remember her, just her hands and her cooking. William never remarried. He raised me in the Ninth Ward, in New Orleans, in the same house his father built.

INTERVIEWER: When did you leave home?

SPEAKER: I enlisted in September 2008. I was eighteen and had no idea what I was doing. I signed up right after 9/11 changed how everybody around me thought, though it took me years to actually go. Basic training was at Fort Bragg. That's where I met my buddy Marcus, who's still my closest friend. We went through everything together.

INTERVIEWER: Was there someone who shaped you in the service?

SPEAKER: My sergeant, Ellis. He was hard on me in a way that I needed. He'd say I reminded him of his own kid. I read a lot in those years too, mostly history, a lot of Malcolm X, which changed how I saw myself and where I came from. Ellis actually gave me my first book.

INTERVIEWER: And after you got out?

SPEAKER: I came back to New Orleans. It took adjusting. About two years ago I finally felt settled. Last spring I even started teaching a class at the community center, which William would've gotten a kick out of. I met my wife Sarah around then, at a mutual friend's cookout. Sarah's brother Danny served too, so we had that in common right away, and he vouched for me, which mattered.

INTERVIEWER: Do you have kids?

SPEAKER: One so far. My daughter, we named her after my grandmother, Ruthie. She's the light of my life. I'm in my mid-forties now, older dad, but I wouldn't trade it. Sarah says Ruthie already has William's stubbornness, which tracks. I want her to know where she comes from, the same way Will made sure I knew.

INTERVIEWER: Do you still see Marcus?

SPEAKER: Every week. He lives across the river now. Danny comes around too, and Ellis and I still talk on the phone, if you can believe it, all these years later. That's the thing about the people who carry you, you don't really let them go.

INTERVIEWER: What do you want Ruthie to understand someday?

SPEAKER: That she's part of a long line of people who didn't have much but gave everything. William, Ruth, all of them. That the Ninth Ward is not just a place we survived, it's a place that made us. I tell her stories about her great-grandfather every night, even though she's too little to follow them. She'll grow into them.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you for sharing all this.

SPEAKER: Thank you for asking. Not many people do.

INTERVIEWER: You've talked before about your recovery. Can you walk me through it?

SPEAKER: Yeah. It started getting bad in my early twenties. I was twenty-two when I first tried to get clean, and it didn't take. My mom, Diane, never gave up on me, even when I gave her every reason to. She's in her late fifties now and still worries like I'm a teenager.

INTERVIEWER: Who else was in your corner?

SPEAKER: My sister Kayla, mostly. She'd drive two hours to sit with me. Kayla's son Elijah was just a baby then, and holding him gave me something to fight for. I have a brother too, we call him Chip, but he was going through his own stuff, so he couldn't be there the same way. No hard feelings. Everybody's fighting something.

INTERVIEWER: Was there a turning point?

SPEAKER: Three years ago. I finally checked into a program in Baton Rouge. My counselor Dana ran the group sessions, and for the first time I actually talked. My doctor, Dr. Nguyen, got my meds right, which mattered more than people understand. And my sponsor, Ray, he calls me every morning still. Ray's saved my life more times than he'll admit.

INTERVIEWER: Where were you living then?

SPEAKER: Back in New Orleans, in a halfway house. There was a woman who ran it, the one who took me in during that stretch, Miss Bea. She wasn't family, but she treated us like we were hers. She'd cook Sunday dinner for anybody who showed up. I don't know where I'd be without her.

INTERVIEWER: Did anything outside of the program help?

SPEAKER: Sounds silly, but I saw Oprah talk about addiction on TV once, really honestly, and it cracked something open in me. Made me feel less like a monster. During COVID it got hard again, being isolated, but by then I had the tools. Dana had drilled them into me. Ray kept calling.

INTERVIEWER: How's the family now?

SPEAKER: Better than I deserve. Kayla and I are close. Elijah's ten now and calls me his favorite. Diane cries every time I hit another year clean. Chip and I are rebuilding, slowly. It's real. I've been chasing this feeling of being trusted again, and it's finally coming back.

INTERVIEWER: What do you tell people who are where you were?

SPEAKER: That you can't do it alone, and you're not supposed to. Find your Diane, your Miss Bea, your Ray. I've been in and out of that darkness since I was a kid, honestly, and the only thing that ever worked was letting people in. Dr. Nguyen told me once that healing isn't a straight line. I hold onto that on the bad days.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you for being so open.

SPEAKER: It's the least I can do. Somebody was open with me once, and it saved me.

INTERVIEWER: Tell me about growing up in the neighborhood.

SPEAKER: We lived on Desire Street, right off MLK Boulevard, in New Orleans. My father James ran a repair shop out of the garage, but everyone knew him as Big Jim. Nobody called him James except the bank. Big Jim could fix anything. My mama Ruby kept the books and kept us all in line. She was the real boss.

INTERVIEWER: Big family?

SPEAKER: Huge. I have my twin brothers, Caleb and Josh, two years younger than me and identical, still can't tell them apart on the phone. My cousin Deborah practically lived with us; we call her Debbie. Debbie and I are the same age, born back in June 1975, so we came up like twins ourselves.

INTERVIEWER: What was the shop like?

SPEAKER: Loud, greasy, wonderful. Jim taught all of us to work with our hands. Caleb took it over eventually. Josh went a different way, into music, moved out to Lafayette. Every year, right before Christmas, Jim would fix up bikes for the kids on the block for free. That was Big Jim. Folks came from all over Desire Street.

INTERVIEWER: When did things change?

SPEAKER: When Katrina hit. We lost the shop, the house, everything on MLK Boulevard. Ruby held us together through it. A couple years ago we finally got a plaque put up where the shop used to be. Caleb pushed for that. I'm in my forties now and I still drive past that lot.

INTERVIEWER: Did you marry, have kids of your own?
