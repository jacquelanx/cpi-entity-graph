INTERVIEWER: Thank you for making the time. I know the season's about to start. Could we begin with where your family is from, originally?

SPEAKER: Originally, we're from Vietnam. From a fishing village outside Saigon, down in the Mekong Delta. My father, Bao, had a boat there, a small wooden thing, nothing like what we run now. My mother, Hoa, she mended nets and sold what he caught at the market before the sun was full up. That was the whole life, and it was a good life, until it wasn't. I was born there, on September 3rd, 1968, so I was only a child when everything came apart.

INTERVIEWER: You left in 1975?

SPEAKER: The spring of 1975, yes. When Saigon fell, my father knew we couldn't stay. He'd done some work for the South, small things, but enough. I was seven. I remember the boat more than I remember the leaving, if that makes sense. We were at sea eleven days. My little brother, Minh — Sonny, everybody ended up calling him Sonny, I don't even know who started it — he was a baby then, and my mother kept him under her shirt the whole time so the sun wouldn't take him. There were forty of us on a boat meant for maybe fifteen. We ran out of water on the sixth day. A tanker finally saw us and radioed, and that's the only reason I'm sitting here.

INTERVIEWER: Where did you land?

SPEAKER: We landed eventually in the Philippines. Spent almost two years in a camp on Palawan before a Catholic charity sponsored us over. People think a refugee camp is a stopping place. It isn't. Children were born there. Old people died there. My mother started sewing again in that camp, mending uniforms for the aid workers in exchange for extra rice, and that's a thing she carried the rest of her life — you make yourself useful, you don't wait to be pitied.

INTERVIEWER: And that brought you to the Gulf Coast.

SPEAKER: To Biloxi, first. There was already a Vietnamese community there, other boat people, and the church helped. Our Lady of Fatima — that parish was everything to us. Father Nguyen, no relation, it's just about the most common name we have, he found my father his first work on somebody else's shrimp boat. Deckhand. My father, who'd captained his own boat, forty-something years old by then, out there as a deckhand for a man half his age. He never once complained where I could hear him. My mother took in sewing, she was a seamstress, the best on the coast, and between them they saved everything that wasn't food or rent.

INTERVIEWER: How old were you when you understood what they'd done to get you here?

SPEAKER: Oh, not until I was grown. I was maybe nineteen, maybe twenty. You don't see your parents as people until later — until you're carrying something heavy yourself. By twenty-three I was working the docks. I could head shrimp faster than any man out there, and I made sure they knew it. We bought our first boat when I was in my late twenties. The Marie Le. Named after a woman who'd been kind to my mother in the camp, a woman we never saw again. License was MS-2287-C. I still know it by heart the way you know a dead person's phone number.

INTERVIEWER: Tell me about the community in those years.

SPEAKER: Close. Tight. And not always welcome, I'll be honest with you. There were people down here, old Cajun families, the Landrys next door being the great exception — Mr. Landry taught my father the local waters, where the shrimp ran in August, which passes to trust and which to leave alone. But others resented us. Said we worked too hard, undercut them, didn't belong. There was ugliness — boats cut loose in the night, words painted on the hulls. My aunt Thao, my mother's sister, she came over in '81 and she cried every week that first year. She'd say, we crossed an ocean to be hated in a new language. My uncle Hai — that's my father's brother — he told her, give it ten years. And he was right, more or less. Time and weddings and funerals, that's what makes a place yours. You bury enough of your people in a ground and it becomes your ground.

INTERVIEWER: You mentioned two people named Hai?

SPEAKER: One uncle Hai, my father's brother. There was also a Hai down the road, ran the ice house, no relation to us at all. People got the two of them confused for years, which they both found insulting for different reasons. That's a small community for you — a handful of names shared out among everybody, and everybody knowing everybody's business.

INTERVIEWER: What was it like raising children between two worlds?

SPEAKER: Hard in ways I didn't have words for at the time. At home we spoke Vietnamese, ate at the family altar, kept the holidays by the lunar calendar. Then the children walked out the door every morning into a country that wanted them to be something else, faster. Trang used to be embarrassed by the smell of the kitchen on her clothes, by me, by all of it. She'd make me drop her a block from the school so the other kids wouldn't see the truck with the shrimp cooler in the back. I understood it, even when it cut me. My mother didn't understand it at all — she'd say, in Vietnam a child who was ashamed of her mother would be talked about for a hundred years. Two different countries in one kitchen, every single night.

INTERVIEWER: Did that change as they got older?

SPEAKER: It always does, if you live long enough. The year Trang turned sixteen she asked me to teach her my mother's fish sauce, the real way, the way you can't buy. She stood at that stove for three hours getting it wrong and wouldn't quit. That was the turn. Now she's the one keeping all of it — the recipes, the prayers, the stories she rolled her eyes at. The children run away from you and then one day they turn around and run just as hard back toward the thing they ran from. I've seen it in every family on this coast, ours and the Landrys' both. Khanh took longer. Boys do. But he calls his grandmother every Sunday now, in Vietnamese, and his own children can't understand a word of it, and they sit and listen anyway because they can tell it's love.

INTERVIEWER: Let's talk about the storms. Katrina.

SPEAKER: Hurricane Katrina took the Marie Le. Took the house, took the dock, took my mother's sewing machine that she'd carried since the camp — that machine had crossed an ocean and it drowned in a parking lot. We were living in Bayou La Batre by then, over the Alabama line, and the water came up twelve feet. My husband, Duc, and I put our kids — my daughter Trang was maybe twelve, and my son Khanh a couple years younger — we put them up in the attic with the photo albums and a jug of water and we prayed in two languages. Governor Barbour came through afterward for the cameras, stood right where our dock had been. Anderson Cooper was down here for a week with the CNN trucks. And then they all left, and we were still standing in the mud with a rake.

INTERVIEWER: How did you rebuild?

SPEAKER: Slowly. There was a caseworker, Ms. Boudreaux, from the diocese, who fought for us with the insurance people when we had no fight left. My English was good by then but Duc's wasn't, and she'd sit with him at the kitchen table for hours going line by line, and she never once made him feel small about it. I think of her as family now. About six years ago we finally paid off the second boat, the one we bought to replace the Marie Le. Stood on the dock and Duc said, well, now we own something the bank can't take. And then — you know what came next.

INTERVIEWER: The oil.

SPEAKER: The BP oil spill. 2010. And that was worse, in a way, than the hurricane, because a hurricane ends. You can rebuild from a hurricane. The oil just sat out there in the Gulf of Mexico and killed the season, and the next season, and the one after that. You couldn't sell what you caught. You couldn't catch it. President Obama came down and made promises, and I believe he meant them. Some of the money even came. Most of it went to lawyers and to men who'd never owned a boat. A couple summers back the catch finally felt normal again, whatever normal is now, but by then a lot of the young people had already gone. My Khanh's in Mobile now, works as a welder, good money, indoors. Doesn't want the water. After everything, I couldn't ask him to want the water. I don't blame him one bit.

INTERVIEWER: Does anyone in the next generation stay with shrimping?

SPEAKER: Trang does, God help her. She's stubborn like my father, like his father I'd guess. She runs the whole operation off her telephone now — the orders, the fuel prices, the weather, all of it on that little screen. She started a page for the business, it's @lebateaushrimp, and people from as far as Atlanta order from us direct. Last spring she took me out at dawn, just the two of us, and the water was flat and pink and I cried like an old woman for no reason I could name. My mother's still with us, in her eighties now, sharp as a fishhook. She sits on the porch and tells the great-grandchildren about the boat and the eleven days and the water they ran out of. The little ones don't believe her. Why would they. They've never once been thirsty.

INTERVIEWER: If people wanted to reach your family — for the record, we'll keep whatever you'd like private.

SPEAKER: Oh, it's a business, we're easy to find. The shop line is 228-555-0143. Email's nguyenfamilyshrimp@example.net. Trang answers faster than I do — I still like to hear a person's voice.

INTERVIEWER: You said earlier that funerals make a place yours. Have there been a lot?

SPEAKER: Too many, and each one a little of the old country going into Mississippi ground. My father passed in the winter, pushing forty years we'd been here by then. My uncle Hai a few years after him. Father Nguyen buried them both out of Our Lady of Fatima, same words, same incense. That little church has married and buried three generations of us now. When my father went, the whole shrimping fleet blew their horns coming into harbor, one after another down the channel. Cajun boats, Vietnamese boats, didn't matter anymore. That's a sound I'll hear till the day I die.

INTERVIEWER: What do you want people to understand about your family?

SPEAKER: That we're Vietnamese and we're from here, both, and there's no contradiction in it, no matter how the news likes to slice people up. My grandchildren are Mississippi kids with Vietnamese names. They eat gumbo and they eat phở and they'd think you were strange for thinking that was strange. My mother crossed an ocean in a boat with a baby under her shirt so that they could argue about football and complain about the heat like everybody else on this coast. That's the whole point. That was always the point. You don't do all that so your grandchildren can be exceptional. You do it so they can be ordinary, and safe, and bored, and here.

INTERVIEWER: That's beautiful. Thank you.

SPEAKER: Thank you for listening. Most people, they want the sad part and then they're gone before the rest. You stayed for the rest.

INTERVIEWER: I appreciate you having me out. Maybe we start with your people — how far back does your family go in these mountains?

SPEAKER: Oh, we've been in southern West Virginia longer than anybody's kept records. Mingo County, mostly, up around Matewan and Red Jacket. My people are Scotch-Irish, mostly, the way most everybody around here is, though my Mamaw Opal always claimed we were part Cherokee on her mother's side. Nobody ever proved it and nobody ever disproved it, which is exactly how those stories go up a holler. You are what your grandmother says you are, and you don't argue with her.

INTERVIEWER: And coal, I take it, is the through-line.

SPEAKER: Coal is everything here. It's the ground and the money and the dying, all three. My father, Earl, went in the mines at fourteen. His father before him. My Papaw Clarence lost three fingers in a roof fall before I was born and worked another twenty years after with what was left of that hand. That's not a hard-luck story to us. That's just Tuesday. You'd be hard-pressed to find a man of my father's generation with all ten fingers and both lungs.

INTERVIEWER: You said your father started at fourteen. What year would that have been?

SPEAKER: He was born March 4th, 1951, so that'd be the middle sixties when he went down. By then the big union days were mostly behind us. My Papaw used to talk about the old fights — the Matewan massacre, back in 1921, when the miners went to war with the coal company's hired gun thugs, right there on the main street of Matewan, men dead in the mud in front of the hardware store. And John L. Lewis, my Papaw would say that name like a prayer, like scripture. The union man. When Kennedy came through, campaigning, in nineteen and sixty, my Papaw shook his hand outside the Williamson courthouse and swore he'd never wash that hand again, and to hear my Mamaw tell it he very nearly didn't.

INTERVIEWER: Your mother — what was her life like?

SPEAKER: My mother, Ruby, she held the whole thing together with baling wire and prayer. Six kids and a husband who came home black to the eyeballs every night, coughing before he was forty. She was a schoolteacher before she married, taught at a little two-room school over in Logan. She had the most beautiful handwriting you ever saw — she'd write our names in the front of the school books like they were something valuable. When my father was on strike, and there were long strikes, months of them, she's the one who figured out how to feed six children on nothing but pinto beans and what came out of the garden. I never knew we were poor until somebody from off told me so.

INTERVIEWER: How many brothers and sisters?

SPEAKER: Six of us, and I'm right in the middle, which means nobody ever paid me much mind, which suited me fine. My oldest brother Denver went off to Detroit in the early seventies to work the auto plants — a lot of our people did, there's whole neighborhoods up there and over in Columbus that are just displaced West Virginians, churches that sing the same hymns we do. My sister Loretta — we all called her Sissy, still do, she's near seventy and she's still Sissy — she stayed. She's the one takes care of Mamaw now, day and night. Mamaw's ninety-one and won't hear a single word about a home. Says she'll die in the house she was married in, and knowing her, she will, just to prove a point.

INTERVIEWER: And you? Did you go into the mines?

SPEAKER: I did. Course I did. What else was there. Went in at eighteen, came out at fifty-five with a back that don't work and lungs that don't neither. I've got the black lung, same as my father, same as his father. Filed the claim years ago — my claim number's 5-338-2201, I know it better than my own birthday, I've said it into so many telephones. They fight you on it, every step. They fought my Papaw, they fought my father to his grave, and Dr. Combs, who's treated three generations of miners out of a little clinic in Williamson, he'll tell you the same thing I'm telling you. He's in his fifties now and he says in all his years he has never once seen the company simply admit that a man's lungs came from their mine. Not once. They'd sooner pay ten lawyers than one widow.

INTERVIEWER: You mentioned foremen earlier, off tape. Was there one who stood out?

SPEAKER: The foreman when I started was a man named Bill Ratliff. Hard man, but a fair one, and down here that's the highest thing you can say about a boss. He'd been down there thirty-odd years ago when the roof came down over on the Red Jacket Number 9, and he crawled back in through the dust and pulled two men out with his own hands when the company men were still standing at the portal deciding what to do. My uncle Bill — a different Bill, my mother's brother — he was on that crew, and he never would talk about it, not once, not in fifty years. Two Bills, and everybody in the county kept them straight but me.

INTERVIEWER: Let's talk about the water. I understand flooding shaped a lot of these hollows.

SPEAKER: The Buffalo Creek flood. That's the one nobody down here will ever forget, the one that gets into your dreams. A coal slurry dam the company swore was safe let go one morning, and the whole hollow washed away, houses and churches and people both, all down the creek at once. That was before I was grown, but my mother lost cousins in it, whole families she'd known since girlhood, gone between breakfast and noon. And then later the mountaintop mining came, and they'd blast the very tops off the mountains and the rain had nowhere left to go but straight down on top of us. The Tug Fork would come up over its banks near every spring and put our road under. We're a people used to water taking things. We're used to the mountain itself being turned against us.

INTERVIEWER: How did the industry's decline hit the family?

SPEAKER: Slow, and then all at once, the way a man goes broke. Back in the eighties, when Reagan came in, that's about when we started feeling the ground shift under us — the union getting broke, the good contracts going. Then the mines mechanized, two men doing what forty used to, and then the Great Recession, and after that they started closing the deep mines for good. My nephew, thirty-seven years old now, strong as an ox, smart as a whip — he hasn't had steady work in years. And that's when the pills came in, into the empty place where the work used to be. We lost two boys from this family to the pills. I won't say their names into your machine, out of respect for their mamas, who are still living and still grieving. A few years ago it got to where it seemed like every other house on our road had a funeral wreath on the door.

INTERVIEWER: Is there still a community holding on through all that?

SPEAKER: The church, mostly. What's left of it holds through the church. Freewill Baptist, down on the creek road, white clapboard, been there a hundred years. Brother Estep's been the preacher there going on forty years, and he'll tell you himself he buries far more than he baptizes now, but he keeps the doors open and the heat on and the coffee going. After 9/11 he had the whole county packed into that little building, people who hadn't darkened a church door in twenty years, all of us needing somewhere to put the fear. That's the thing about a place like this. When things are good, folks scatter. When things are bad, they remember exactly where the church is.

INTERVIEWER: Did you ever think about leaving, like your brother Denver did?

SPEAKER: Thought about it more than once. My wife wanted to, early on, before the children came. There was talk of Columbus, a fellow I knew had work lined up in a warehouse, good pay, regular hours. But my mother was still living then, and my Mamaw, and around here you don't leave your people to save yourself. That's not a rule anybody says out loud. It's just in you. Now I'm sixty-eight and I couldn't leave if I wanted to — this is the only ground that knows my name. They can call it Appalachia and study us like we're some foreign country they discovered, send their reporters and their photographers, but it's home. And every one of them wants the same sad story, the toothless-hillbilly story, the poverty story. Not one of them ever wants the part where we took care of each other through all of it.

INTERVIEWER: If a researcher wanted to follow up with you — only what you're comfortable sharing.

SPEAKER: I'm not hard to find, never have been. Phone's 304-555-0176. My grandson set me up one of them email addresses a while back — it's hollowridge51@example.org — though I'll confess I check it about once a month whether it needs it or not. He runs some kind of a page too, posts the old photographs of the mines and the men, and he calls it @coalcountry_grandkids. The young ones, that's how they keep the memory now, on the telephone. Used to be we did it on the porch, passed it mouth to ear. Now it's a screen. I don't know that it's worse. It's just different, and I'm too old to say which.

INTERVIEWER: One last thing. What do you want people outside these mountains to understand?

SPEAKER: That we fed this country. Every light that ever came on in Charleston or in Washington or anywhere else in this nation, some man down a hole in Mingo County paid for it with a piece of his lungs. We are not a problem for somebody to solve. We are the people who kept the lights burning for a hundred years and got left in the dark ourselves the minute the coal got cheaper somewhere else. My Papaw Clarence, my father Earl — they were not victims and they were not fools and they were not ignorant. They were the strongest and the most honorable men I have ever known or ever will, and they died young and they died coughing. You remember that part. Anybody can remember the poverty. You remember the strength. That's the part that matters, and that's the part they always leave out.

INTERVIEWER: I will. Thank you.

SPEAKER: You drive safe getting back down off this mountain. It's a good deal further than it looks, and the fog comes up quick this time of evening.
