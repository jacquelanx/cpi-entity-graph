SPEAKER: My dad admired Obama, used to say America gave him a fair shot. He worked as a janitor, cleaning offices at night, and did landscaping during the day. My mom took care of houses. INTERVIEWER: How did the restaurant go?

SPEAKER: Slow at first. We had regulars, though. There was a customer named Grace who came in every single day for years. She'd sit at the counter and tell us about her grandkids. INTERVIEWER: Is the family close now?

SPEAKER: Very. Roberto's wife, Elena, does the books for the restaurant. She's sharp, way better with numbers than either of us. My niece Camila, their daughter, works weekends at the register. She's twelve, so mostly she just charms the customers. My mom, she's in her sixties now, still comes in to supervise, whether we want her to or not.

INTERVIEWER: Any traditions you've kept?

SPEAKER: Sunday meals, always. Lucia cooks the old recipes. There's a photo of Beyonce on the wall, my niece put it up, she's a huge fan. And we keep a picture of my father near the register. He passed a few years ago, but it still feels like he's watching the place.

INTERVIEWER: Where did you grow up before LA?

SPEAKER: A bit in Chicago, honestly back when I was a teenager it's a blur. We moved so much. But Los Angeles is where we planted roots. My kids were born here. I want them to know where Hector started, cleaning those offices, so they never take it for granted.

INTERVIEWER: What would your dad think of the restaurant now?

SPEAKER: He'd be proud, I think. He'd probably tell me to work harder anyway. That was Hector. Lucia says I sound just like him when I lecture the staff. Maybe she's right. Family is the whole thing for us. Everything we built, we built together, and every plate that goes out has a little of that history in it. That's what I'd want people to understand about us.

INTERVIEWER: How can people find the restaurant?

SPEAKER: Come by, or call 213-555-0147. We're on Instagram now too, @betos_kitchen, Camila set it up. You can email us at hola@betoskitchen.com for catering.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you so much.

SPEAKER: Of course. Anytime.

INTERVIEWER: Let's talk about who raised you.

SPEAKER: My grandfather William did, mostly. Everyone called him Will, or Will Senior when my dad was around. He was a quiet man, a machinist. My grandmother passed when I was young, her name was Ruth, and I barely remember her, just her hands and her cooking. William never remarried. He raised me in the Ninth Ward, in New Orleans, in the same house his father built.

INTERVIEWER: When did you leave home?

SPEAKER: I enlisted in September 2008. I was eighteen and had no idea what I was doing. I signed up right after 9/11 changed how everybody around me thought, though it took me years to actually go. Basic training was at Fort Bragg. That's where I met my buddy Marcus, who's still my closest friend. We went through everything together.

INTERVIEWER: Was there someone who shaped you in the service?

SPEAKER: My sergeant, Ellis. He was hard on me in a way that I needed. He'd say I reminded him of his own kid. I read a lot in those years too, mostly history, a lot of Malcolm X, which changed how I saw myself and where I came from. Ellis actually gave me my first book.

INTERVIEWER: And after you got out?

SPEAKER: I came back to New Orleans. It took adjusting. About two years ago I finally felt settled. Last spring I even started teaching a class at the community center, which William would've gotten a kick out of. I met my wife Sarah around then, at a mutual friend's cookout. Sarah's brother Danny served too, so we had that in common right away, and he vouched for me, which mattered.

INTERVIEWER: Do you have kids?

SPEAKER: One so far. My daughter, we named her after my grandmother, Ruthie. She's the light of my life. I'm in my mid-forties now, older dad, but I wouldn't trade it. Sarah says Ruthie already has William's stubbornness, which tracks. I want her to know where she comes from, the same way Will made sure I knew.

INTERVIEWER: Do you still see Marcus?

SPEAKER: Every week. He lives across the river now. Danny comes around too, and Ellis and I still talk on the phone, if you can believe it, all these years later. That's the thing about the people who carry you, you don't really let them go.

INTERVIEWER: What do you want Ruthie to understand someday?

SPEAKER: That she's part of a long line of people who didn't have much but gave everything. William, Ruth, all of them. That the Ninth Ward is not just a place we survived, it's a place that made us. I tell her stories about her great-grandfather every night, even though she's too little to follow them. She'll grow into them.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you for sharing all this.

SPEAKER: Thank you for asking. Not many people do.
